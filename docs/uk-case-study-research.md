# UK Case Study Page Research — Senior Product Designer (Design Systems / B2B Enterprise SaaS)

Research date: 15 September 2026. Scope: London / UK product design market, mid–senior–lead, plus UK Global Talent Visa (Tech Nation) evidence use.

Confidence key used throughout:
- **[P]** primary source (hiring manager writing, company careers page, gov.uk / Tech Nation)
- **[S]** secondary / aggregator (Glassdoor-derived process descriptions, SEO explainers) — directionally useful, verify before relying on it
- **[D]** sources disagree — flagged explicitly

---

## 0. Executive summary — the 12 rules that matter

1. **Your case study is read in three passes, not one: ~30 seconds visual, ~3 minutes structural, then a real read.** A hiring manager at Feedly who screened 800+ portfolios for one senior role reports ~70% fail the 30-second visual check and ~90% never get past the 3-minute structural review ([micka.substack.com](https://micka.substack.com/p/what-really-matters-when-hiring-product)) **[P]**. Design the page for pass 1 and 2 first.
2. **Outcome goes above the fold, before process.** Lead with what changed, then the story ([leslieyang.substack.com pt.1](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in)) **[P]**; GDS explicitly ranks "the results of your work, for both users and for organisations" as the *first* thing its panel looks for ([designnotes.blog.gov.uk](https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2020/07/01/how-to-create-a-portfolio-to-apply-for-a-design-role-in-government/)) **[P]**.
3. **3–5 case studies, no more.** Independently converged on by GDS ("3 to 5 case studies") **[P]**, Leslie Yang ("3–4") **[P]**, and Tom Scott's 2026 hiring FAQ ("3–5 focused case studies") **[P]** ([verifiedinsider.substack.com](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/faq-design-hiring-in-2026)).
4. **Order matters more than completeness.** GDS: "people are most likely to look at the first 2–3" **[P]**.
5. **Write in first person singular.** GDS: "Use 'I' instead of a third-person perspective" **[P]**. Leslie Yang lists "using 'we' instead of claiming individual contributions" as a rejection trigger **[P]**.
6. **3–5 sentences per section, not paragraphs** (Leslie Yang) **[P]**; GDS says "short paragraphs of 3 to 4 sentences" per STAR element **[P]**. Target 600–900 words per case study; The Fountain Institute's 2026 synthesis says 500–800 **[S]**.
7. **Under a 10-minute read, total.** Matej Latin's rejection list includes "case studies exceeding 10-minute read time" ([matejlatin.com](https://matejlatin.com/blog/only-30-seconds-to-reject-your-portfolio/)) **[P]**.
8. **Show craft at pixel level, not just narrative.** Feedly's hiring manager rejects "distant mockups without detailed close-ups" **[P]**; Leslie Yang requires UI legible "no squinting or zooming needed" **[P]**; Tom Scott says visual craft is mandatory at IC level and a "purely UX background" struggles at portfolio review **[P]**.
9. **Cut the Double Diamond diagram.** Process-methodology diagrams now read as junior/templated; hiring managers want evidence you *lived* the process ([thefountaininstitute.com](https://www.thefountaininstitute.com/blog/senior-product-designers-hired)) **[S/P]**.
10. **Seniority = initiative + product thinking + scope + innovation**, in that priority order (Leslie Yang pt.2) **[P]**. Scope means naming team size, cross-functional partners, stakeholder count.
11. **NDA work is normal in UK enterprise hiring and there is an official UK playbook for it** — GDS: "make the work anonymous. Don't disclose the organisation" and describe the industry/approach at a higher level **[P]**.
12. **For the Global Talent Visa, a case study is never evidence on its own.** Tech Nation's 2025 tightening demands third-party validation and quantifiable indicators; evidence must show *your personal* impact, not the company's ([immigram.io](https://www.immigram.io/blog/2025-tech-nation-updates-optional-criteria-oc1-oc2-oc3-and-oc4)) **[S]**, ([getendorsed.co.uk](https://getendorsed.co.uk/blog/understanding-tech-nation-criteria-2026)) **[S]**.

## 1. What UK hiring managers and design leads actually look for

### 1.1 The screening funnel (numbers you can design against)

| Stage | Time | Fail rate | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visual first impression | ~30 sec | ~70% eliminated | [micka.substack.com](https://micka.substack.com/p/what-really-matters-when-hiring-product) **[P]** |
| Structural skim (can I find problem / role / outcome?) | ~3 min | ~90% cumulative | same **[P]** |
| Full read | 5–10 min | — | [matejlatin.com](https://matejlatin.com/blog/only-30-seconds-to-reject-your-portfolio/) **[P]** |
| GDS sift → interview | panel scores against "Who you are" criteria | top **5–7 applicants** progress | [designnotes.blog.gov.uk](https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2022/03/02/how-gdss-design-recruitment-process-works/) **[P]** |

Practical test, from the same body of advice: get someone to scan the page for 30 seconds, close the tab, and say what they remember. If they can state **problem, your role, outcome**, the page works ([blog.opendoorscareers.com](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-to-write-a-strong-case-study-for-your-portfolio-in-2025-a14b)) **[S]**.

### 1.2 GDS — the most explicit published UK criteria

GDS is the only UK employer that publishes both its portfolio rubric and its recruitment mechanics. Even if you never apply to government, it is the cleanest statement of UK expectations, and it leaks into every UK public-sector-adjacent, agency and consultancy hiring panel.

What the GDS panel looks at, **in order** ([source](https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2020/07/01/how-to-create-a-portfolio-to-apply-for-a-design-role-in-government/)) **[P]**:
1. the results of your work, for both users and for organisations
2. the different approaches you used — including what worked and what didn't
3. the problems you were solving
4. your reflections on your approach

Note what is *absent*: tooling, visual polish of the portfolio itself, and methodology names. GDS explicitly says **"Don't over-design the portfolio itself. Put the focus on the work."** **[P]**

Other hard GDS rules **[P]**:
- 3–5 case studies; strongest first
- STAR framework, 3–4 sentence paragraphs per element
- first person "I"
- plain English, no jargon
- max 25 pages if submitting a file; landscape PDF; contents page; image descriptions
- anonymise NDA work, don't name the organisation

GDS recruitment mechanics ([source](https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2022/03/02/how-gdss-design-recruitment-process-works/)) **[P]**:
- application = CV + cover letter (up to ~750 words / 2 pages) + **portfolio link**; the portfolio link "will form part of assessing your application"
- applications are **not anonymised**, precisely because "portfolios are an essential part of the recruitment process"
- 1.5-hour remote interview: 20 min prep + 10 min answer on a work scenario, **15 min presenting 2 projects from the portfolio**, 25 min of 5 behavioural questions, 10 min candidate questions
- assessed against Civil Service **Success Profiles** and the **DDaT capability framework for user-centred design** — and your assessed capability level affects the allowance you're paid
- "We are as interested in their approach and methods as we are in the finished designs"

### 1.3 What causes rejection (consolidated)

From Matej Latin **[P]**, Leslie Yang **[P]**, Feedly/Micka **[P]**, Tom Scott **[P]**:

- Dribbble/Behance gallery links with no context — "I will move on to another applicant's portfolio" (Leslie Yang)
- "We" instead of "I"; unclear personal contribution
- Generic case study titles ("Company Name Onboarding")
- Pixelated / small / un-zoomable images; distant mockups with no close-ups
- Spelling and grammar errors (matters more in the UK than candidates expect — GDS and agencies read the cover letter as a writing sample)
- Cookie-cutter template structure
- Walls of text; paragraphs where bullets belong
- No stated outcome, or vague success metrics bolted on at the end
- Portfolio hosted on a free subdomain, or as a raw Figma prototype / Notion page (Feedly) **[P]** — note this is one person's bar, treat as a signal not a law
- Portfolio not linked from LinkedIn (Feedly) **[P]**
- Student/fantasy redesigns; work older than ~2 years presented as headline work
- Anything you can't defend under pressure — Tom Scott: the common failure is "a gap between what they say and what they can show"

### 1.4 Seniority signals specifically (mid → senior → lead)

Leslie Yang's four signals, priority-ordered ([pt.2](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in-d25)) **[P]**:

1. **Initiative** — you wrote or co-wrote the brief with your PM; you didn't wait for a complete brief.
2. **Product thinking** — you separated root problem from feature gap; you made data-informed decisions.
3. **Scope** — an explicit slide/section stating team size, cross-functional partners, number of stakeholders, and where your work sat in the wider product.
4. **Innovation** — at least one project that pushed the envelope, with what made it into scope and what didn't.

"Initiative and product thinking are more important" because they take years to develop. For a design-systems candidate this maps cleanly: initiative = you proposed the system/governance model; scope = number of squads, products and engineers consuming it.

Tom Scott adds two 2026-specific seniority filters **[P]**:
- candidates who "commit to a direction without exploring alternatives" fail
- candidates who only operated in "well-defined lanes" (PM defines the problem, designer executes) read as mid-level regardless of title

## 2. Structure patterns that work now — and what changed

### 2.1 The shift: outcome-first, not chronology-first

The old chronological "Discover → Define → Develop → Deliver" case study is now a negative signal. The replacement pattern is **result in the first paragraph, story afterwards** — e.g. "Redesigned the onboarding flow, increasing activation by 34%" as the opening line, then how ([dribbble.com/resources](https://dribbble.com/resources/career/product-design-case-study)) **[S]**, echoed by Leslie Yang **[P]** and GDS's results-first rubric **[P]**.

Supporting structural devices that are now expected rather than optional:
- A **TL;DR / summary block** at the top: role, team, timeframe, problem in one line, outcome in one line.
- **Descriptive titles** in the form "How I [action] to [result]" rather than "<Company> Design System" ([matejlatin.com](https://matejlatin.com/blog/only-30-seconds-to-reject-your-portfolio/)) **[P]**.
- **40/60 text-to-visual ratio** (acceptable range 25/75 to 50/50) — Matej Latin **[P]**.
- Interactive proof: clickable prototype, Figma link, or short video walkthrough — Tom Scott calls these "essential, not optional" for 2026 **[P]**.

### 2.2 What specifically changed in 2025–2026

**a) Template saturation / "process theatre".** The persona named Sally, the sticky-note photo, the perfect Double Diamond, the clean prototype — this sequence now signals *school work* rather than real work. Hiring managers want evidence you lived the process, not that you rehearsed it ([thefountaininstitute.com](https://www.thefountaininstitute.com/blog/senior-product-designers-hired)) **[S/P]**. **[D]** — the claim circulating as "UX Collective analysed 10,000 portfolios and 72% were identical" appears only in low-quality aggregators and I could not trace it to a UX Collective publication. **Do not cite that statistic.** The underlying qualitative point is well-supported; the number is not.

**b) AI-generated case study fatigue.** The objection is not AI use, it's unedited AI voice and ownership ambiguity: "generic AI-generated case studies often describe actions without clearly stating who made decisions or what role the designer played." Feedly's hiring manager lists **AI-generated cover letters** as an explicit red flag **[P]**. The counter-move is specificity — named trade-offs, named constraints, named things you rejected. See §5.3.

**c) AI fluency as a hiring axis.** Figma-sourced figures via The Fountain Institute: 73% of design managers say AI tool proficiency is increasingly necessary; 79% prioritise candidates who can design AI products; one UK hiring manager told Figma that prioritising AI proficiency "completely changed the way they hire" **[S]** (second-hand; treat the percentages as indicative). Practical read for a design-systems candidate: a short, honest note on where you used AI in the systems work (token migration scripts, codegen, docs) and what judgement you applied reads better than silence.

**d) Portfolio walkthroughs being partly replaced by live exercises.** 25-minute live Figma challenges in first rounds are increasingly common; portfolio walkthroughs are sometimes skipped entirely **[S]**. The written page therefore has to survive being read *without you in the room* — see §6.4.

**e) AI-assisted screening before human review.** Reported at ~78% of recruiters **[S]**. Implication: the case study page needs machine-readable, plain-language text (real HTML headings and body copy, not text baked into images), and role/skill keywords present in prose.

**f) Market pressure.** UK context: average employer receiving ~140 applications per vacancy, a 59% year-on-year rise **[S]**; junior/mid design openings reported at 500–800 applicants **[S]**. Meanwhile 82% of design leaders say demand for designers is flat or up **[S]**. Net effect: selectivity, not scarcity — which is exactly the environment where a skimmable, outcome-first page wins.

### 2.3 Reconciling the frameworks

Three named structures appear in the sources. They are compatible:

- **STAR** (Situation, Task, Actions, Result) — GDS's official recommendation **[P]**, also Matej Latin's **[P]**. Best for the *written* page in a UK context because it's the same frame Civil Service behavioural interviews use.
- **Problem → Approach → Outcome** — the common private-sector shorthand.
- **Minto Pyramid** (answer first, then supporting arguments) — the mechanism behind "outcome-first" **[S]**.

Recommendation: **STAR content, Minto order.** Write STAR, then invert so the Result sits at the top as a summary block and repeats in full at the bottom.

## 3. NDA / enterprise B2B work where outcomes aren't public

This is the single most under-solved problem for B2B/enterprise candidates, and it is where the UK has the clearest official guidance.

### 3.1 The rules

**GDS (official UK government position)** ([source](https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2020/07/01/how-to-create-a-portfolio-to-apply-for-a-design-role-in-government/)) **[P]**:
> "If you have work created under non-disclosure agreements, make the work anonymous. Don't disclose the organisation."

…and describe the industry, the approach and the work at a higher level. GDS also says you may blur sensitive images. GDS's 2022 recruitment post confirms this is an accepted, non-penalised route: for confidential work, "applicants describe projects without identifying details" **[P]**.

**IxDF** ([source](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/how-to-handle-non-disclosure-agreements-ndas-when-you-write-your-ux-case-study)) **[P/S]**:
- Publicly-shipped product = always safe. Paywalled or internal = get permission.
- Replace raw numbers with percentages: "a 12% increase in monthly signups", not "12,512 → 14,013".
- Zoom out: a wide shot of a workshop wall instead of legible Post-its.
- Ask permission explicitly, naming *which* projects, *what* content, and *where* it will be shown; send the final draft for approval.
- Offer password-protected case studies or a private link for recruiters, with a short public summary of your role.

**[D] Direct conflict on redaction.** IxDF says **do not blur or black out** — it still breaches the NDA if discovered, risks missing something, and "signals untrustworthiness to recruiters." GDS says blurring sensitive images is acceptable. Resolution for a UK enterprise candidate: **prefer redrawing over redacting.** Rebuild the screen with neutral placeholder data and a generic brand — this satisfies both positions, and it is what the "abstract visuals" advice across sources actually means in practice.

### 3.2 The B2B-specific moves that work

1. **Anonymise the client, not the domain.** "A North American bulk-materials ERP used by ~X dispatch teams" is far stronger than "a large enterprise client". The domain is what makes you credible for another enterprise role; the logo is not.
2. **Describe the constraint set instead of the product.** Enterprise constraint sets are distinctive and non-confidential: legacy data model, permission matrices, multi-tenant theming, 15-year-old table interactions, regulated audit trails, users who are trained rather than self-serve, desktop-only. Hiring managers read constraint literacy as seniority.
3. **Show the artefact, not the customer's data.** Component anatomy, token architecture, variant matrices, contribution/governance model, docs pages, before/after of the *system*, not of the customer's screen. This is the design-systems candidate's advantage: your deliverable is often intrinsically non-confidential.
4. **Substitute internal-audience outcomes for customer-facing ones.** Adoption, coverage, handoff time, defect counts, number of consuming teams — all internal, generally shareable, and directly relevant. See §4.
5. **Two-tier page.** Public tier: problem, constraints, your role, approach, redrawn visuals, directional outcomes. Gated tier: the real screens and real numbers, released on request. State explicitly at the top: *"Client details and figures are under NDA. Detail available on request under discussion."* That sentence is itself a positive signal — it reads as commercial maturity rather than as a gap.
6. **Get written permission anyway.** Even where no NDA exists, a one-line email approval from a former employer is the standard professional courtesy and protects you (IxDF) **[P/S]**.

### 3.3 What not to do

- Don't lead with "I can't show this due to NDA" as the first line — it front-loads a negative in the 30-second window.
- Don't black-bar screenshots (see §3.1 conflict).
- Don't substitute a fictional concept project for the enterprise work and hope it reads as equivalent. In 2026 "fantasy redesigns without real constraints" are an explicit rejection trigger **[S/P]**, and for a *senior* enterprise candidate the concept project actively undercuts the seniority claim.

## 4. Showing measurable impact when metrics are thin

Metrics matter more the more senior you are — this is consistent across every source. But "metric" is broader than "revenue".

### 4.1 Acceptable proxies, strongest first

| Tier | Proxy | Why it's accepted |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Adoption / coverage**: % of screens or components on the system, number of consuming products/squads, % of tokens replacing hardcoded values | Directly measurable, internal, non-confidential; the canonical design-system metric **[S]** |
| 1 | **Cycle-time**: design→dev handoff time, time to build a new screen, number of design-QA defects per release | Verifiable internally, reads as business value |
| 2 | **Behaviour change**: task completion rate, time-on-task, error rate, support-ticket volume on the affected flow | Framed as user behaviour, no revenue disclosure needed **[S]** |
| 2 | **Directional / relative figures**: "roughly halved", "~12% increase", "cut two steps from a seven-step flow" | Explicitly endorsed for NDA work **[P/S]** |
| 3 | **Decision impact**: the roadmap changed because of your research; a feature was cut; a build/buy decision reversed | Leslie Yang's "initiative" signal **[P]** |
| 3 | **Durability**: the framework, governance model or component library the team still uses after you left **[S]** | Proves the work outlived the project |
| 4 | **Scope as a proxy**: team size, stakeholder count, number of workflows/product areas covered | Leslie Yang's explicit "scope" signal **[P]** |
| 4 | **Qualitative attributions**: a named quote from the PM/engineering lead about what changed | Third-party validation; doubles as visa evidence (§8) |

### 4.2 Rules for using proxies honestly

- **Never invent numbers.** State the measurement that existed. If none existed, say what you'd have measured and why it wasn't instrumented — that admission reads as senior, not weak.
- **Attribute honestly.** Tech Nation's standard is a good discipline generally: evidence must demonstrate *your* personal impact, not the company's **[S]**. Write "the flow I designed" not "the company grew".
- **Pair every number with its baseline and its window.** "12% increase" alone is noise; "12% over the first quarter after rollout, against a flat prior six months" is evidence.
- **If you have no outcome at all, say what you learned and what you'd change.** GDS's fourth-ranked criterion is literally "your reflections on your approach" **[P]** — in the UK this is scored, not tolerated.
- **A shipped redesign that moved nothing is worth as much as a concept project** — so if the metric is flat, the case study's value has to come from the decision quality, not the visuals **[S]**.

## 5. Visual and editorial presentation

### 5.1 The balance

- **40% text / 60% visual** is the recommended default; the tolerated range is 25/75 to 50/50 **[P]** ([matejlatin.com](https://matejlatin.com/blog/only-30-seconds-to-reject-your-portfolio/)).
- **Craft must be legible.** Leslie Yang requires UI shown so no squinting or zooming is needed **[P]**. Feedly's hiring manager wants "specific components and close-ups rather than distant mockups" **[P]**.
- **The portfolio must not out-design the work.** GDS: "Don't over-design the portfolio itself. Put the focus on the work." **[P]** This is a genuinely UK-flavoured note — over-art-directed portfolios read as agency/visual-designer positioning, which can work against a product-design application.

### 5.2 The three failure modes, named

1. **Dribbble-style mockup dump** — floating device mockups, no context, no role. Leslie Yang closes the tab **[P]**.
2. **Wall of text** — paragraphs instead of 3–5 sentence blocks; 10+ minute read **[P]**.
3. **Process theatre** — the Double Diamond diagram, the persona, the sticky-note photo, the affinity map, presented as the substance rather than as evidence **[S/P]**. For a senior enterprise candidate this is the most dangerous of the three, because it's the one that reads as *junior* rather than merely as *unclear*.

A fourth, specific to design-systems candidates: **component-sheet dump** — twelve screenshots of a Figma library with no adoption story, no governance, no consumer. It has the same effect as a mockup dump.

### 5.3 Editorial voice — the anti-AI-tell checklist

Since generic prose now reads as machine-written, the differentiators are all specificity:
- Name the constraint, the system, the year, the team, the tool, the version.
- Name at least one thing you were wrong about and one thing you rejected.
- Name the disagreement — with the PM, with engineering, with a stakeholder — and how it resolved.
- Use British English consistently (colour, organise, prioritise, CV not resume) — matches the market and signals the page was written, not generated with US defaults.
- Avoid the register that reads as generated: "leveraged", "seamless experience", "delved into", "robust solution", sentence-per-line rhythm.

## 6. UK-specific hiring mechanics and what they demand of the written page

### 6.1 Process shapes at named UK employers

| Employer | Process (as published/reported) | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **GDS / gov.uk** | CV + ~750-word cover letter + portfolio link → panel sift, top 5–7 progress → 1.5h interview: scenario question (20 min prep / 10 min answer), **15 min presenting 2 projects**, 5 behavioural questions (25 min). Scored against Success Profiles + DDaT UCD framework. ([source](https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2022/03/02/how-gdss-design-recruitment-process-works/)) | **[P]** |
| **Monzo** | 30-min intro call → **45-min+ portfolio walkthrough** (1–2 projects, end-to-end process and deliverables) → prep call → final round of 3 interviews on **craft, impact, teamwork** (~2.5–3h over 2 days). Monzo's own hiring blog notes non-engineering roles use "a portfolio review of a recent project that demonstrates the end-to-end process and results", and take-homes are timeboxed to **no more than 4 hours**. ([posthog.com handbook](https://posthog.com/handbook/people/hiring-process/design-hiring)) | **[P]** |

### 6.2 What this means for the *written* page

1. **Two of your case studies must be presentable in 15–20 minutes each.** GDS gives 15 minutes for *two* projects; Starling gives 90 minutes for two; Leslie Yang budgets ~20 min per project. Build the page so its section headings are the spine of a talk.
2. **Craft / impact / teamwork is a real UK rubric** (Monzo). Make sure each case study has an explicit paragraph for each of the three. Teamwork especially — UK panels probe collaboration harder than US ones, and the Civil Service behavioural format formalises it.
3. **Written communication is scored.** GDS reads a 750-word cover letter; PostHog explicitly weights writing. Your case study prose *is* the writing sample. This is an argument against a visuals-only portfolio.
4. **The page must work unattended.** Revolut reviews files without a presentation; AI screening runs before humans. Anything that only makes sense when you narrate it is invisible in those paths.
5. **Expect a take-home; expect it to be contested.** A Design Week poll found **74% of respondents agreed design tests used in hiring are unfair or exploitative** ([designweek.co.uk](https://www.designweek.co.uk/do-unpaid-interview-tests-exploit-designers/)) **[P]**. Better UK employers respond by paying (PostHog's paid SuperDay) or timeboxing (Monzo's 4-hour cap). A strong, defensible written case study is your best argument for *skipping* or shortening a take-home — several teams will waive it if the portfolio already evidences the thing the exercise tests.
6. **Visa status.** If you need sponsorship, say so plainly on the site (or say you hold/are applying for a Global Talent visa — which requires no sponsor and is a genuine competitive advantage worth stating). Many UK job ads filter on this at sift.

### 6.3 Tone calibration for the UK

- **Understate rather than overstate.** US-style superlatives ("transformational", "10x") land badly with UK panels; specific, modest, verifiable claims land well.
- **Credit the team, claim your decisions.** The UK failure mode is the opposite of the US one: British candidates over-use "we". Use "I" for decisions, "we" for delivery, and be explicit about the split.
- **British English throughout.**
- **"CV", not "resume."**

### 6.4 Design-systems-specific UK note

Design systems is one of the few specialisms where UK job ads are explicit about the artefact. London senior/lead ads routinely require "creating and maintaining design systems and token libraries" and "expert knowledge of Figma (auto layout, components, variables)" **[S]**. Implication: one case study should be a *systems* case study with governance and adoption, and one should be a *product/feature* case study proving you still ship end-to-end — the common rejection of systems specialists is "can they still do product work?"

## 7. Accessibility and performance of the portfolio site itself

### 7.1 The UK legal baseline (useful as content, not just as compliance)

- **WCAG 2.2 Level AA** is the required standard for UK public sector websites and apps under the *Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) (No. 2) Accessibility Regulations 2018* ([gov.uk guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/accessibility-requirements-for-public-sector-websites-and-apps)) **[P]**. The GOV.UK Service Manual states services must meet WCAG 2.2 AA as a minimum, work with screen magnifiers / screen readers / speech recognition, and publish an accessibility statement ([service manual](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/helping-people-to-use-your-service/making-your-service-accessible-an-introduction)) **[P]**.
- **All UK service providers** — private sector included — have a duty to make reasonable adjustments under the **Equality Act 2010** (Disability Discrimination Act 1995 in Northern Ireland) **[P]**.
- **European Accessibility Act** became enforceable **28 June 2025**. UK businesses selling to EU consumers in scope sectors (e-commerce, banking, telecoms, transport, AV media, e-books) with ≥10 employees or >€2m turnover are caught; WCAG 2.2 AA gives presumption of conformity ([levelaccess.com](https://www.levelaccess.com/compliance-overview/european-accessibility-act-eaa/)) **[S]**. This is highly relevant to a B2B SaaS designer: it's a live commercial driver you can reference in a case study.

### 7.2 What this means for your own site

The portfolio is read as a work sample. Concretely:

- Target **WCAG 2.2 AA on your own site** and be able to say so. The nine new 2.2 criteria that most often break designer portfolios: visible focus appearance, dragging alternatives, target size (minimum), consistent help, redundant entry, and accessible authentication ([NHS service manual summary](https://service-manual.nhs.uk/accessibility/new-criteria-in-wcag-2-2)) **[P]**.
- **Keyboard-navigable** in full: case study navigation, image lightboxes, video controls, and any custom scroll/animation.
- **Real text, not text-in-images**, for headings and body copy — needed for screen readers *and* for AI/ATS screening (§2.2e).
- **Alt text on every case study image**, written as description of the design decision, not "screenshot". GDS explicitly asks for image descriptions in portfolios **[P]**.
- **Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`** — an animated intro that can't be stopped is a visible failure for a designer claiming systems/accessibility competence.
- **Contrast** — the most commonly failed criterion on designer portfolios; light-grey-on-white body copy is a self-inflicted wound.
- **Performance**: heavy hero video / uncompressed PNGs are read as indifference to UX. Keep case study pages fast on a mid-range phone and on hotel wifi; reviewers open portfolios on phones between meetings **[S]**.
- **Optional but strong**: a short, honest accessibility statement page for your own site. In the UK this reads as fluency with the actual regulatory regime, which is a differentiator for public-sector-adjacent, fintech and health roles.

⚠️ Counter-note: don't let accessibility theatre become its own process theatre. One line ("Built to WCAG 2.2 AA; tested with VoiceOver and keyboard only") beats a page about it.

## 8. Case studies as UK Global Talent Visa (Tech Nation) evidence

### 8.1 The frame

Digital Technology Global Talent is endorsed by **Tech Nation** on behalf of the Home Office; decisions typically 5–8 weeks for endorsement ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/global-talent-digital-technology)) **[P]**. You apply as **Exceptional Talent** (established, 5+ years) or **Exceptional Promise** (≤5 years) ([technation.io](https://technation.io/global-talent-visa/)) **[P]**. Product/UX designers apply on the **business** side of the digital technology route (commercial, investment or **digital product expertise**), not the technical side ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/global-talent-digital-technology)) **[P]**.

### 8.2 Hard format rules that govern how a case study can be submitted

From gov.uk ([documents you need](https://www.gov.uk/global-talent-digital-technology/documents-you-need-to-apply-endorsement)) **[P]**:

- **Up to 10 pieces of evidence** total.
- **At least 2 pieces** for the mandatory criterion.
- **At least 4 pieces** for exactly **2 optional criteria** (2 per criterion).
- **Each piece: maximum 3 A4 pages.**
- **You cannot reuse the same evidence for two criteria.**
- **You may not combine documents** — each piece is one document, clearly summarised.
- Plus: application form, **personal statement**, **CV (max 3 A4 pages)**, and **3 letters of recommendation** (each up to 3 single A4 pages excluding the author's credentials; dated, typed, signed, with the author's CV/credentials).

### 8.3 The criteria (gov.uk wording)

**Mandatory:** "you've been recognised by others as being a leading talent in the digital technology field in the last 5 years" (Exceptional Talent) / potential leader (Exceptional Promise) **[P]**.

**Optional (pick 2)** ([gov.uk eligibility](https://www.gov.uk/global-talent-digital-technology/eligibility)) **[P]**:
- **OC1** — record of innovation as a founder or senior executive of a product-led digital technology company
- **OC2** — record of innovation in a new digital technology field or concept **as an employee**
- **OC3** — contribution to the digital technology sector **outside of work** (mentoring, collaborative projects, speaking, open source, writing)
- **OC4** — set up or worked in a product-led digital technology company with technical, commercial or entrepreneurial contributions

⚠️ **[D] Numbering conflict.** gov.uk's on-page ordering and the numbering used by Tech Nation's own guide and by immigration advisers differ: advisers commonly label OC1 = innovation, OC2 = recognition beyond your occupation, OC3 = significant technical/commercial/entrepreneurial contribution, OC4 = academic contribution ([getendorsed](https://getendorsed.co.uk/blog/understanding-tech-nation-criteria-2026), [immigram](https://www.immigram.io/blog/2025-tech-nation-updates-optional-criteria-oc1-oc2-oc3-and-oc4)) **[S]**. **Always work from the current Tech Nation Application Guide PDF, not from a blog's numbering.** The substance of the four buckets is stable; the labels are not.

For a senior B2B/design-systems designer the realistic pairing is **the "significant contribution" criterion + the "recognition beyond your occupation" criterion**.

### 8.4 How design work is actually used as evidence

Per adviser summaries of Tech Nation's guidance for product/design applicants **[S]**, acceptable evidence in the significant-contribution bucket includes:
- documentation of product designs or architecture diagrams **clearly showing your contribution**
- **screenshots of what you worked on, including Figma screenshots** and product-launch screenshots
- a supporting reference letter from a CPO or equivalent
- commercial outcomes (revenue, funding, users, adoption) **directly tied to decisions you made**

### 8.5 The 2025 tightening — this is the part that changes how you write

Reported changes to the optional criteria in 2025 **[S]** ([immigram](https://www.immigram.io/blog/2025-tech-nation-updates-optional-criteria-oc1-oc2-oc3-and-oc4)):
- **Quantifiable indicators required** — "straightforward, quantifiable indicators" of how your work affected the company, product or market.
- **Third-party validation weighted heavily** — especially validation from qualified people; self-reported achievement is weak evidence.
- **Personal contribution must be isolated** from team/company achievement.
- **Mentoring must be in person** — ADPList/Codementor-style online mentoring reportedly no longer counts on its own.
- **Publications need editorially-reviewed platforms** — self-publishing carries a stigma relative to vetted venues. ⚠️ This matters directly: **a case study on your own portfolio site is self-published and therefore weak as standalone evidence.**

### 8.6 Practical rules for making your case studies visa-useful

1. **A portfolio case study is a supporting exhibit, never the evidence itself.** Package it as: a 3-page PDF containing (a) a one-paragraph summary of your contribution, (b) 2–4 design artefacts/screenshots with captions naming *your* decisions, (c) the quantified outcome — and pair it with an independent letter or an external artefact that corroborates it.
2. **Build every case study so it can be cut to 3 A4 pages, landscape, with captions that stand alone.** This constraint is compatible with the hiring-market constraint (600–900 words, skimmable) — design once, use twice.
3. **Engineer corroboration while you still work there.** A signed letter from a CPO/VP Design stating "Kate designed and led the X design system, adopted by N teams, reducing Y by Z%" converts a self-published case study into real evidence.
4. **Design-systems work maps unusually well to the "significant contribution" criterion** — it is internal, measurable, attributable, and demonstrably infrastructure others depend on ("does this exist without my specific contribution?" is the test advisers report **[S]**).
5. **Feed the "recognition beyond your occupation" criterion deliberately**: conference talks at vetted venues, articles on editorially-reviewed platforms (Smashing Magazine, A List Apart, an established design publication — *not* your own blog), open-source or public design-system contributions, judging, in-person mentoring with documentation.
6. **NDA work is not disqualifying for the visa either** — the same anonymisation approach applies, but corroborating letters carry proportionally more weight because you can't show the artefact publicly.
7. **Timeline**: Tech Nation recommends up to ~12 weeks to prepare, then up to 8 weeks for the endorsement decision and ~3 weeks for the visa application **[P]**. Build the evidence pack alongside the portfolio, not after.

## 10. Recommended section-by-section skeleton with word counts

Target: **600–900 words of body copy** per case study page (≈6–8 minute read with visuals), 40% text / 60% visual. Two of your case studies must also work as a 15–20 minute talk.

### Above the fold — the 30-second layer (~90–120 words + 1 hero image)

| Element | Words | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **Title** | 8–14 | "How I [action] so that [outcome]". Not the product name alone. |
| **One-line outcome** | 15–25 | The result, stated plainly, with baseline and window. If confidential: directional + "figures under NDA". |
| **Fact strip** | 30–45 | Role · My contribution in one clause · Team composition · Timeframe · Platform/context · Constraint headline (e.g. "regulated, desktop-only, 15-year-old data model"). |
| **NDA note (if applicable)** | 12–20 | One sentence, neutral, offering detail on request. |
| **Hero visual** | — | A real, legible screen or a system artefact — not a floating device mockup. |

### The 3-minute layer — headings the reader skims

**1. The problem (80–120 words)**
What was broken, for whom, and why it was worth solving. Business context in the first sentence, user context in the second. One number if you have one. No personas.

**2. Constraints and what made it hard (70–110 words)**
Enterprise candidates: this is your highest-value section and it costs you nothing under NDA. Legacy systems, permission models, multi-tenancy, regulation, trained-vs-self-serve users, engineering capacity, org politics. 4–6 bullets is acceptable here.

**3. My role and scope (60–90 words)**
Explicitly: what you decided, what you delivered, team size, cross-functional partners, number of stakeholders, number of consuming teams/products. First person. This is the seniority slide — do not fold it into a byline.

**4. Approach — 2 to 4 decision blocks (250–380 words total, 70–100 each)**
Not "Discover / Define / Develop / Deliver". Instead, name the **decisions**:
- *Decision block format:* the choice you faced (1 sentence) → the options (1–2) → what you chose and why (1–2) → the visual that proves it (1 image with a caption naming the decision).
- At least one block must contain a trade-off you *lost* or a direction you *killed*.
- Show iteration inside one block (v1 → why it failed → v2), not as a separate "iterations" gallery.

**5. Craft close-up (40–70 words + 2–4 tight images)**
Component anatomy, token structure, states/variants matrix, spec detail, motion. Captions carry the argument. Required for IC-level roles — visual craft is assessed even for systems specialists.

**6. Outcome (80–130 words)**
Repeat the headline result in full. Then:
- primary metric with baseline and measurement window
- 1–2 proxy metrics (adoption, coverage, cycle time, defect count, consuming teams)
- one third-party attribution if you have it (a quote from the PM or eng lead)
- if metrics are thin, say what wasn't instrumented and what you'd measure next — explicitly, not apologetically

**7. Reflection (50–80 words)**
What you'd do differently; what the work taught you; what survived after you left. GDS scores this. Keep it specific and unflinching — one real mistake beats three polite ones.

**8. Footer (20–40 words)**
Links: live product if public, prototype, spec/docs page, talk or article if any. A line offering the confidential detail under discussion. Next/previous case study.

### Layout rules

- Every heading must make sense read alone, in sequence, with no body copy. That sequence is your interview talk track.
- Nothing load-bearing inside an image. Text stays as text (screen readers + AI screening).
- Max ~2 images per scroll-height; each image gets a caption that states a decision, not a description.
- Mobile: the fact strip and the outcome must both be visible without scrolling on a ~390px viewport.

### Portfolio-level (the index page)

| Element | Words | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Positioning line | 12–20 | Specific, not categorical: "Senior product designer — design systems for complex B2B SaaS", not "designer & problem solver". Specificity is what makes the skim productive. |
| Case study cards | 25–35 each | Title + one-line outcome + 2–3 tags (domain, discipline, scale). Strongest first. |
| Availability / visa line | 10–15 | Location, right to work / Global Talent status, what you're looking for. |
| Contact + CV (British English: "CV") | — | One click, above the fold or in the nav. |

### Suggested portfolio composition for this profile

1. **Design system case study** — governance, adoption, multi-product scale. Your headline.
2. **Complex B2B feature, end-to-end** — proves you still ship product, not only infrastructure. Closes the "systems specialist can't do product" objection.
3. **A hard constraint / legacy-redesign story** — the enterprise credibility piece.
4. *(Optional)* **A craft-forward or exploratory piece** — shorter, visual-led, proves range without diluting the first three.
